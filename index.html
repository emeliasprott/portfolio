Selected work

Institutional Networks in California Lawmaking

California lawmaking produces records across several systems that describe different pieces of the same process: bills, votes, committees, legislators, lobbying, and campaign finance. I built a 20-year relational database linking those records, then used the resulting network in a graph-based model of bill passage and institutional influence. The project asks whether relationships among legislators, organizations, money, and bills help explain why legislation moves through the process the way it does.

  • Graph neural networks
  • Relational data modeling
  • Legislative analytics

AI and the Division of Labor

This project looks at how AI is changing the division of work between people and machines. I distinguish between search-style tasks, where an answer can be evaluated almost immediately and is therefore more amenable to automation, and tasks that depend on tacit knowledge, judgment, or context that cannot be separated so easily from the person doing the work. The analysis uses that distinction to examine where AI is likely to substitute for labor, where it is more likely to augment it, and how those differences may reshape the value of human capital across occupations.

  • Labor economics
  • Human capital
  • Technological change

Housing and Economic Adjustment in the Bay Area

The Bay Area has gone through several very different housing and economic shocks since 2008 without a comparable amount of change in the housing stock itself. I combine mortgage, census, property, housing, and regional economic data to study how that relatively fixed stock is reallocated as purchasing power, financing conditions, household geography, and the position of existing homeowners change. The project follows the market from the housing crash through the 2010s expansion, pandemic, and recent increase in mortgage rates, with particular attention to who can enter the market, who remains in place, and how decisions made in one period shape the market that exists in the next.

  • Housing markets
  • Household finance
  • Regional economics

Twitter Suspensions and Misinformation

In January 2021, Twitter removed a large number of accounts associated with misinformation over a six-day period, creating a useful distinction between accounts that were suspended and similar accounts that remained on the platform. I use the activity of those remaining accounts, along with external news and event data, to estimate how much misinformation the suspended accounts would likely have shared had they remained active. I then separate that directly removed activity from changes among the users who remained, allowing the analysis to distinguish the immediate effect of account removal from broader changes in misinformation sharing after the suspensions.

  • Synthetic difference-in-differences
  • Causal inference
  • Experiment design

Understanding the whole system.

In my opinion, data makes more sense when understood within in terms of the system that generated it. Data science provides the technical foundation for my work, and political economy shapes how I frame questions and interpret evidence.

I pay close attention to the relationships and incentives behind a dataset, then use that context to build analysis that is both methodologically sound and practically useful. I am most drawn to work where the difficult part is not only technical execution, but deciding what question to ask and what the available evidence can reasonably support.
